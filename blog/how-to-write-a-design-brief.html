To write a design brief the designer gets right the first time, answer eight things in writing before work starts: the goal, the audience, the key message, every format and size, the content, the references, the deadline, and the one person who approves. Most revision rounds are not caused by bad design. They are caused by information that was missing from the brief and arrived later as feedback. This guide on how to write a design brief gives you the fields, a template and the mistakes to avoid.

Key takeaways

  • A good design brief answers eight questions: goal, audience, message, formats, content, references, deadline and approver.
  • Unclear requirements are a well-documented cause of failed work: PMI found that 47% of unsuccessful projects missed their goals because of inaccurate requirements management (PMI Pulse of the Profession, 2014).
  • List every format up front. Sizes added after the first draft are one of the most common reasons a deadline slips.
  • Name one approver. Feedback from several people who disagree costs more time than any design change.
  • Short is fine. A clear five-line brief beats a long document nobody finished.

What is a design brief?

A design brief is a short written description of what needs to be designed, why, for whom and by when. It is the designer's single source of truth, and it is what you check the result against.

A brief is not a creative direction document or a mood board. It can include references, but its main job is to remove guesswork. If the designer has to guess the goal, the audience or the sizes, the first draft is a guess too.

Why do design requests go wrong?

Design requests usually go wrong because key information is missing at the start, not because the designer misunderstood a clear brief. Missing information doesn't disappear. It returns as feedback on the first draft, when changing direction is expensive.

The pattern I see most often in a design queue:

  1. A request arrives as one line in a chat: "Can we get a banner for the launch?"
  2. The designer makes reasonable assumptions about size, message and tone.
  3. The first draft goes to three people. One wants a different headline, one wants a different product photo, one asks for Instagram story and LinkedIn formats too.
  4. The second round fixes the first round's feedback and reveals new disagreements.

None of this is a design problem. It is a briefing and approval problem. PMI's research on requirements points the same way: nearly half of unsuccessful projects miss their goals because of inaccurate requirements management. The data comes from project management, not design specifically, but the mechanism is the same.

What should a design brief include?

A design brief should include eight fields. Each one answers a question the designer would otherwise have to guess.

FieldQuestion it answersWeak exampleStrong example
GoalWhat should this piece achieve?"Promote the launch""Get demo bookings from the launch ad campaign"
AudienceWho will see it, and what do they already know?"Our customers""Marketing directors at mid-size e-commerce brands who haven't heard of us"
Key messageWhat is the one thing they should remember?"We're the best""Set up in one day, no developer needed"
Formats and sizesWhere will it appear, in which sizes and languages?"Social banner""LinkedIn 1200×627, Instagram story 1080×1920, EN and ES"
ContentWhat text, images, logos and data must appear?"Use the usual stuff""Headline and CTA below, product screenshot attached, logo from the brand folder"
ReferencesWhat should it look like, or not look like?none"Like our last webinar banner; avoid stock photos of people"
DeadlineWhen is the final version needed, and when is it used?"ASAP""Final by Thursday 12:00, campaign starts Monday"
ApproverWho gives the final yes?"The team""Head of Marketing (one named person), final decision"

If you only have time for three fields, write the goal, the formats and the approver. These three prevent most rework.

How do you write a design brief step by step?

Write a design brief by answering the eight fields in order, attaching the content, and confirming the approver before you send it. Here is the process:

  1. Start with the goal in one sentence. If you can't write it, the request isn't ready yet.
  2. Describe the audience in one line, including what they already know about you.
  3. Write the key message as the visitor should remember it, not as an internal tagline.
  4. List every format, size and language now. Check with the channel owners before you send the brief.
  5. Attach final content: copy, product images, logos, data. Mark anything that is still a placeholder.
  6. Add one to three references, and say what you like about each. "Like this" without a reason is hard to use.
  7. Give two dates: when you need the final file, and when it goes live.
  8. Name one approver, and agree with them before the brief goes out.
  9. Read it as the designer. Could someone outside your company start work without asking a question?

The last line matters more than it looks. "No people in the image", "don't mention price" or "avoid our old logo" saves a full round of revisions.

What are the most common design brief mistakes?

The most common design brief mistakes are missing formats, unnamed approvers, placeholder content and vague goals. Each one costs a revision round or a missed deadline.

  • "ASAP" as a deadline. Every request becomes urgent, so none of them is. Give a real date and say whether it can move.
  • Formats added later. A layout built for a wide LinkedIn banner rarely works as a vertical story. Adding formats after approval often means redesigning, not resizing.
  • Feedback from a committee. When several stakeholders comment separately and disagree, the designer ends up mediating. Collect feedback, resolve conflicts, then send one consolidated list.
  • Placeholder copy treated as final. If the headline changes after design, the layout often changes too.
  • References without reasons. Three references with three different styles leave the designer to pick one, and it may not be the one you meant.
  • No success criteria. Without a goal, feedback turns into personal taste.

How does a better brief reduce revision rounds?

A better brief reduces revision rounds because feedback shifts from "this isn't what we meant" to small refinements. When the goal, formats and approver are clear, the first draft is already in the right direction.

Vague briefClear brief
First draftBased on assumptionsBased on agreed goal and content
Round 1 feedbackDirection change, new formatsSmall copy and visual refinements
Round 2 feedbackNew opinions from new reviewersFinal approval
Risk to deadlineHighLow

A fixed number of revision rounds helps too. When each round is limited, teams tend to consolidate comments and give clearer, more specific feedback.
